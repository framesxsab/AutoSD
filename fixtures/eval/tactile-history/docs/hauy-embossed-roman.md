# Valentin Hauy and Embossed Roman Print

Valentin Hauy founded the first school dedicated to blind students in Paris in 1784 after noticing a blind musician reading engraved musical cues. Hauy reasoned that blind pupils could read the same printed books as sighted peers if paper were embossed deeply enough from the reverse side using specially cast mirror-image metal type.

His students pressed damp, heavy rag paper against oversized italic Roman letterforms to create raised outlines along the page surface. While this proved to skeptics that blind students could master geography, grammar, and literature, curved serif alphabets turned out to be slow and tiring to distinguish by touch. Fingers recognize sharp spatial dot constellations far faster than subtle differences between O, Q, C, and G curves. Embossed Roman books remained expensive, bulky, and impossible for students to write by hand without heavy printing presses.
